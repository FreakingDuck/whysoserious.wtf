Lorisse · for Claude Code

Agents write the code. You read three short pages and decide what merges.

Lorisse is a way of working with Claude Code, with a VS Code extension. Agents write the code, each in its own branch; a gate of tests decides whether it holds; you review three short pages a part in place of the code, and nothing merges without your word.

Your agents write faster than anyone can read. Lorisse gives you back the last word. Whether you code yourself or lead the project without touching the code, you stay the one who decides.

What a review looks like

You read a page of the project, never its code. When something puzzles you, you write a note in the page, on a line that starts with two slashes.

Lorisse answers right under your note, so the question and the answer read as one thread. When you agree with the page, you mark it, and your mark is written under its title with the date. That line is the record of what you approved.

What you get

An agent with a memory that outlives the session

Lorisse keeps what it learns about you and the project as small files in a repository of yours. It reads them at the start of every session, you can correct them, and git keeps their history.

Three pages a part, and your marks on them

Each part has a README that says how it works, a contract that lists what it promises, and a validation that says how you check each promise by hand. You mark each page read, ok or to rework, and the mark carries its date.

Guard rails that the tool applies itself

A hook lets an agent run one command per shell call. The permissions are written as lists and checked before they are installed. A part you lock cannot be edited by an agent.

A reader in VS Code

The extension shows every page with its state, tells you in words what changed since your mark, and writes your notes and your marks into the page when you click.

What it adds to Claude Code

Claude Code gives you the engine: memory files, sub-agents, a shell. Lorisse gives it habits you can count on. Every part gets its three pages. Every change meets the gate. A part you lock stays locked. And you have one calm place to read and to mark.

None of this rests on an agent's good memory. The rules live in files and hooks that Claude Code runs on every call, so they hold on the day the agent is tired of them too.

One change, from your ask to the merge

Here is one change to a made-up unit converter.

  1. You askYou say what you want, in your own words: "a temperature below absolute zero should be refused".
  2. The promise firstBefore any code, Lorisse writes what will be true: a new clause in the part's contract, or a change to one, with the step that checks it.
  3. A builder in its own branchA sub-agent takes that one part, in a branch of its own, and writes the code and its tests.
  4. The gate says noThe gate runs every test. One fails, so nothing can merge. The builder fixes it, and the gate turns green.
  5. You say mergeThe branch joins the main branch on your word, never before.

Your review, at your own pace

Reading is a loop of its own. You open the reader when you have a quiet quarter of an hour, not when an agent needs you.

  1. You read what changedThe reader lists the pages that moved since your last mark, and says in words what changed in each.
  2. You ask in the pageSomething puzzles you? You leave a note where it is, and Lorisse answers right under it.
  3. You markRead, ok, or to rework. Your mark is written under the title with its date: it is the record of what you agreed to.

By the agent, for the human

The code, the tests, the contracts, the method and this page were written by Lorisse and the build agents it launches, through Claude Code. The owner says what he wants, reads the three pages, walks the steps by hand, and says yes. He does not read the code: the gate does, and the contract says what the code must do in words he can check.

The agent books every pass, every decision and every slip in files, so that the next session, the next machine, or the owner on a Monday finds the same state. Most of the rules exist because something went wrong once. The agent is the author; the owner is the reader.
